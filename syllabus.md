## Description

Tools from artificial intelligence and machine learning, such as deep-learning-based protein
structure prediction, machine-learned interatomic potentials, and language-model agents that
plan experiments, are delivering great value within chemistry, physics, biology, materials
science, and other scientific domains. Problems from the sciences, such as the need to integrate
first-principles knowledge into empirically-driven predictions, are simultaneously inspiring
AI/ML methodology development. This class will introduce students to emerging research and
opportunities at this intersection through discussion of papers.

## Course format

The course is a paper-reading seminar. Each session takes a paper, or a small group of related
papers, and works through it. The instructor will first introduce the background needed to
understand an area in a lecture format, including both the AI/ML methodology and the scientific
context. We will then discuss the papers. Students are expected to read the papers before the
in-class discussion and to offer a few thoughts beforehand via a google form.

Students are welcome from a variety of backgrounds and it is likely that some will have a
stronger AI/ML or mathematical background while others will have a stronger background in a
scientific domain. This will create an opportunity to practice inclusivity, including being
patient when questions are asked and discussing papers in a way that helps everyone in the class
understand and participate.

Students will help build the topic list. The running list is on the
[topics page](topics.html), and suggestions are welcome throughout the semester.

## Topics

Topics may be adjusted during the semester based on time and student interest but are planned to
include:

- machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs)
- protein language models and protein structure prediction (AlphaFold / OpenFold, ESM3)
- discrete diffusion for protein generation
- diffusion and other generative models for design in science
- black-box optimization for design (AlphaEvolve, LLOME)
- agents for science --- AI co-scientist systems, and the language-modeling background needed to understand them
- self-driving labs
- computer vision for characterization and lab robotics
- inverse problems with generative models
- extracting information from the scientific literature

## Intended audience and prerequisites

The course is intended for the following student groups:

- PhD students who are comfortable with AI/ML and python and want to learn about interesting high-impact problems in the sciences
- PhD students from the sciences who have some familiarity with AI/ML and python who would like to learn how to apply these methods within their research
- PhD students working at the intersection of AI/ML with a scientific domain who would like to expand their knowledge of how AI/ML is used in science

Students taking the course are expected to have:

- the ability to write code in python
- knowledge of probability, statistics and machine learning at an undergraduate level (e.g., comparable to ORIE 3120)

No scientific background will be assumed. Scientific concepts will be explained in an accessible
way.

## Project

The course will culminate with an optional computational project that can be done solo or in a
team of two students. Students will have substantial latitude to choose their project. Examples
of appropriate projects include applying an existing AI/ML method to a problem from a scientific
domain (especially one from the student's dissertation topic), developing a new AI/ML method to
address an open scientific challenge, reproducing and expanding the results from a recent
AI/ML-in-science research article via a new implementation, or providing a thorough literature
review of an emerging area at the intersection of AI/ML and a scientific domain. Projects that
have the potential to become new research articles are especially encouraged. Students will be
asked to submit a written project report and present their project in class. Constructive
thoughts will be solicited from the class on the project reports with a goal of helping the
authors refine their work.

Students who choose to do the project will earn 3 credits. Other students will earn 2 credits.

## Assessment

The course will be graded S/U. Grading on assignments will be generous with a focus on competence
rather than perfection but the bar for an S will be higher than usual: to earn an S, students
must get 80% of the points in the course.

For students taking the 3-credit version of the course, points will be awarded according to the
following breakdown:

- Comments on research articles in submitted google forms (50%)
- Project report and presentation (50%)

For students taking the 2-credit version:

- Comments on research articles in submitted google forms (100%)
