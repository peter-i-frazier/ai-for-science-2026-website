## Description

2026 is an exciting time in AI for science. Deep-learning-based protein structure prediction, machine-learned interatomic potentials, language-model agents that plan experiments, and self-driving labs are all accelerating the pace of progress in chemistry, physics, biology, materials science, and other scientific domains. Problems from the sciences, such as the need to integrate first-principles knowledge into empirically-driven predictions, are simultaneously inspiring AI/ML methodology development. Commercially, venture capital, tech companies like Google and Anthropic, and the pharmaceutical industry are all making major investments with the expectation that AI for science will pay massive dividends.

This course brings together students from both mathematical/computational and scientific backgrounds. For students from the sciences, the course offers an accessible introduction to core AI concepts and methods that can accelerate your science. For students with a mathematical or computational background, the course introduces important scientific problems where AI is having an impact and research questions that, if answered, would accelerate this impact.

## Homework

The course is not only a paper-reading seminar. It opens with a crash course in the AI/ML
background the papers assume, and there are four homework assignments through the semester,
designed to reinforce the core ideas. The goal is that a student who arrives with a weak AI/ML
background leaves understanding how these methods actually work.

**Each assignment has two parts.**

**Part A --- no AI assistance.** Short conceptual questions you answer in your own words. These
exist so you can find out what you actually understand. They are graded on evidence of thought
rather than on correctness, so there is nothing to be gained by getting the "right" answer some
other way, and a lot to lose. Expect roughly 30 minutes.

**Part B --- AI assistance encouraged.** The implementation and experimentation. Use whatever
tools you would use in your own research. Because these tools make the mechanical parts cheap,
Part B asks for more than a comparable assignment would have a few years ago.

Note at the top of each submission which tools you used and where. This is not a compliance
exercise. Being explicit about it is a skill worth having.

## Topics

Topics may be adjusted during the semester based on time and student interest but are planned to
include:

- machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs)
- protein language models and protein structure prediction (AlphaFold / OpenFold, ESM3)
- discrete diffusion for protein generation
- diffusion and other generative models for design in science
- black-box optimization for design (AlphaEvolve, LLOME)
- agents for science --- AI co-scientist systems, and the language-modeling background needed to understand them
- self-driving labs
- computer vision for characterization and lab robotics
- inverse problems with generative models
- extracting information from the scientific literature

## Intended audience and prerequisites

The course is intended for the following student groups:

- PhD students who are comfortable with AI/ML and python and want to learn about interesting high-impact problems in the sciences
- PhD students from the sciences who have some familiarity with AI/ML and python who would like to learn how to apply these methods within their research
- PhD students working at the intersection of AI/ML with a scientific domain who would like to expand their knowledge of how AI/ML is used in science

Students taking the course are expected to have:

- the ability to write code in python
- knowledge of probability, statistics and machine learning at an undergraduate level (e.g., comparable to ORIE 3120)

No scientific background will be assumed. Scientific concepts will be explained in an accessible
way.

## Project

The course will culminate with an optional computational project that can be done solo or in a
team of two students. Students will have substantial latitude to choose their project. Examples
of appropriate projects include applying an existing AI/ML method to a problem from a scientific
domain (especially one from the student's dissertation topic), developing a new AI/ML method to
address an open scientific challenge, reproducing and expanding the results from a recent
AI/ML-in-science research article via a new implementation, or providing a thorough literature
review of an emerging area at the intersection of AI/ML and a scientific domain. Projects that
have the potential to become new research articles are especially encouraged. Students will be
asked to submit a written project report and present their project in class. Constructive
thoughts will be solicited from the class on the project reports with a goal of helping the
authors refine their work.

Students who choose to do the project will earn 3 credits. Other students will earn 2 credits.

Everyone reads the papers and submits brief comments beforehand. That is what makes the
discussion work, so it is required at every level. It is a low bar --- a few sentences per paper.

The letter-graded tracks exist for students who want to be held to something. Grading is generous
and focused on competence rather than perfection: **competence plus consistent effort earns an
A-**. Opting into a grade should not be a gamble, and if you are on the fence, it is a reason to
opt in rather than a reason not to.
