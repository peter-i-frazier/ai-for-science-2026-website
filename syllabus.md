## Description

2026 is an exciting time in AI for science. Deep-learning-based protein structure prediction, machine-learned interatomic potentials, language-model agents that plan experiments, and self-driving labs are all accelerating the pace of progress in chemistry, physics, biology, materials science, and other scientific domains. Problems from the sciences, such as the need to integrate first-principles knowledge into empirically-driven predictions, are simultaneously inspiring AI/ML methodology development. Commercially, venture capital, tech companies like Google and Anthropic, and the pharmaceutical industry are all making major investments with the expectation that AI for science will pay massive dividends.

This course brings together students from both mathematical/computational and scientific backgrounds. For students from the sciences, the course offers an accessible introduction to core AI concepts and methods that can accelerate your science. For students with a mathematical or computational background, the course introduces important scientific problems where AI is having an impact, state of the art methods for these problems, and research questions that, if answered, would accelerate this impact.

## Course format

The course will use traditional lectures, class discussions of research papers, homeworks, and a project. The course will be divided into units, each on a different area of AI for science (e.g., protein structure prediction, or machine learned interatomic potentials). Each unit contains (1) one or more lectures on AI methods and scientific background; (2) an in-class discussion of recent research papers; (3) for units involving more difficult-to-grasp AI concepts, a homework assignment that helps solidify these concepts. The course will culminate with a project. Students are expected to read the papers before the in-class discussion and to submit a few thoughts beforehand through Gradescope.

The running list of topics is on the [topics page](topics.html). Additional suggestions are welcome. Meeting dates, the university calendar, the planned unit order, and homework release dates are on the [schedule page](schedule.html).

Students may take the course in one of three ways:
- For 3 credits, graded. All course assignments are required.
- For 2 credits, graded. The project is not required.
- For 2 credits, S/U. These students need not do the homeworks or the projects. They are required to read the papers and submit thoughts before class, for 80% of the paper discussion days.

Students are welcome from a variety of backgrounds and it is likely that some will have a
stronger AI/ML or mathematical background while others will have a stronger background in a
scientific domain. This will create an opportunity to practice inclusivity, including being
patient when questions are asked and discussing papers in a way that helps everyone in the class understand and participate.

## Intended audience and prerequisites

The course is intended for the following student groups:

- PhD students who are comfortable with AI/ML and python and want to learn about interesting high-impact problems in the sciences
- PhD students from the sciences who have some familiarity with AI/ML and python who would like to learn how to apply these methods within their research
- PhD students working at the intersection of AI/ML with a scientific domain who would like to expand their knowledge of how AI/ML is used in science

All students taking the course are expected to have:

- the ability to write code in python
- knowledge of probability, statistics and machine learning at an undergraduate level

No scientific background will be assumed. Scientific concepts will be explained in an accessible
way.
## Homework

Homework will be used to help students practice core ideas, where this practice is beneficial. We intend to have four homeworks in the course.

**Each assignment has two parts.**

**Part A --- no AI assistance.** Questions you should answer on your own. These exist so you can find out what you actually understand and fill gaps.

**Part B --- AI assistance encouraged.** The implementation and experimentation. Use whatever AI tools you would like, as long as you understand the core of what the tools are doing. If someone were to ask you to explain how the code works or what the AI tool is doing, you should be able to do so.

Note at the top of each submission which AI tools you used and where.

The homework is designed to teach core concepts. If you are a senior PhD student working in AI or ML, you may already have a firm grasp of these concepts. If this is the case and you want to take the course for a grade, talk to the instructor.  He may say that you won't benefit much by doing the homework and waive the requirement. In its place, he will ask you to do a more substantial project, targeting something that has a more direct path toward publication and/or can appear in your dissertation.
## Papers

Students will be asked to read the papers discussed in class before the discussion and to submit comments and questions through Gradescope.

Do this work (read the papers and write your comments) without using an LLM.  LLMs are a good tool for getting a quick understanding of many papers but really understanding a paper requires you to actually read it. Afterward, if you would like to get help with something that you didn't understand by asking an LLM to explain something, you may do this. If this changes what you want to say, you can edit your submission.
## Project

The course culminates in a computational project. **It is required only for 3 credits, graded.**
Everyone else is welcome to do one anyway.

### What it is

You pick the problem. Four shapes work well:

- Apply an existing AI/ML method to a problem from a scientific domain --- **especially one from
  your own dissertation.**
- Develop a new AI/ML method for an open scientific challenge.
- Reproduce a recent AI/ML-in-science paper via a new implementation, and push past it.
- Write a thorough literature review of an emerging area at the AI/ML--science intersection.

Projects with a path toward a publishable research article are especially encouraged. If you are
a senior PhD student and I waive your homework, the project is what replaces it, and I will
expect it to be more substantial.

### What you actually produce

| Deliverable | What it is | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Proposal** | Half a page: the problem, the data or simulator, what you will have to show, who you are working with | Fri, Oct 30 |
| **Work session** | One class meeting to work on projects and get feedback | Mon, Nov 23 |
| **Presentation** | In class, to the whole group. Length is set once we know how many projects there are | Mon, Nov 30 -- Fri, Dec 4 |
| **Report** | Written, workshop-paper length --- roughly 6--8 pages plus references | Last week of classes |

Work alone or in pairs. Pairs are expected to produce correspondingly more, and to say who did
what.

After the presentations, the class is asked for constructive thoughts on each report, so that
authors can refine the work. Reading and commenting on your classmates' projects is part of the
project grade.

### Using AI on the project

Same rule as Part B of the homework: use whatever tools you like, and be able to explain what
your code and your tools are doing. Note at the top of the report which tools you used and where.

**Dates and lengths in this section are provisional** and will be confirmed in class before the
proposal is due.

## Recordings

Lectures are recorded over Zoom and posted **to Canvas, where only students registered in the
course can see them.** They are not posted on this public website.

The reason is that the recording captures you, not just me --- your questions, your name in the
participant list, your face if your camera is on. That makes it an education record of an
identifiable student, which under FERPA I should not publish to the open web without your written
consent. Restricting the recordings to the class is the simple way to stay on the right side of
that.

Two consequences worth knowing:

- **Recordings are for people in this class.** Do not repost them, or clips of them, anywhere else.
- **If you would rather not appear,** keep your camera off and ask your questions in the chat or
  after class, and you will not be in the recording. Tell me if you want something you said cut.

Slides are a different matter --- those are mine, and they stay public on the
[schedule page](schedule.html).
