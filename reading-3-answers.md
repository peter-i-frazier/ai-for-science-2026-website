## 1. Explain and judge the three hypotheses (Zhang et al.)

*In your own words, explain Fig. 1 of Zhang et al. and the paragraph that introduces it ("In this work, we dissected how the language model ESM-2 enables..."). Then state the evidence for and against each hypothesis, and judge how strong it is.*

8 answers.

Fig. 1 and its introductory paragraph distinguish three possible mechanisms: hypothesis 1 learns folding physics, pictured as finding a low-energy conformation; hypothesis 2 recognizes a protein family and retrieves its domain- or fold-level coevolutionary model; and hypothesis 3 predicts contacts from smaller interacting sequence motifs and their relative separation, potentially reusing them across families. For hypothesis 1, accurate predictions are compatible with learning physical constraints, but the paper provides no direct demonstration of an energy-based mechanism; against it, the authors report that accuracy depends on training-set sequence neighbors and show that 18 domain-splitting isoforms are generally predicted as confident fragments of their parent structures with exposed aggregation-prone surfaces (Fig. 2A–E). I judge this persuasive evidence against a sufficiently general understanding of folding physics, but insufficient to show that ESM-2 learns no physical principles, especially because the authors lack experimental structural measurements of these isoforms. For hypothesis 2, the parent-like isoform predictions and similarities between ESM-2 and family-alignment coupling weights support evolutionary retrieval (Fig. 4B,E), although neither establishes that retrieval operates at the whole-family level. Against that stronger interpretation, revealing residues around two interacting segments recovers contacts roughly 2.5–3 times more efficiently than revealing the same number randomly throughout the protein, including for widely separated segments (Fig. 6A,B). Hypothesis 3 receives its strongest support from this local-context advantage and abrupt recovery jumps—for example, the SusD contact appears when flank length increases from 13 to 14 residues (Fig. 6C–F). However, these experiments measure recovery of the supervised Contact Head’s original predictions, and contiguous fragments could still identify a whole family; they also do not directly establish independent motif models shared across families. Thus, hypothesis 3 is the best-supported working explanation, with strong evidence that local context can suffice and more limited evidence for the precise internal storage mechanism.

In Zhang et al., the authors set out to dissect how the protein language model ESM2 enables accurate structure prediction from an individual sequence without being supplied an explicit multiple sequence alignment. The introductory paragraph asks whether this single sequence capability means the model has genuinely internalized the biophysical principles of protein folding, or whether it relies on an entirely different mechanism. To test this, Figure 1 presents three competing hypotheses. Hypothesis 1 imagines the model as a physical folding engine, represented by an energy landscape funnel where an unfolded chain navigates free energy minima toward its native state. Hypothesis 2 conceives the model as a global fold lookup library, where an entire sequence is recognized through global homology and mapped onto a discrete, memorized fold category. Hypothesis 3 views the model as a modular lookup table of interacting segment motifs, illustrated by pairs of secondary structure elements such as beta strands pairing together along the sequence to form local contact blocks.

Hypothesis 1, that the model folds proteins using physics, is essentially disproven by the authors' findings. If ESM2 were truly simulating thermodynamic folding in solution, its performance would not strictly depend on the number of evolutionary relatives present in its pretraining data, yet that correlation is exceptionally strong across all model sizes. More decisively, the authors show that when presented with spliced protein isoforms missing essential domains, ESMFold predicts them as folded fragments that match their exact conformation in the full length parent. This leaves large, non physical hydrophobic surfaces exposed to solvent, quantified by high Spatial Aggregation Propensity scores, rather than collapsing or adopting realistic, unfolded conformations. Their unsupervised categorical Jacobian analysis further demonstrates that the model primarily retrieves discrete coevolutionary couplings rather than modeling continuous physical forces, providing nearly conclusive evidence against a physics driven mechanism.

Hypothesis 2, that the model memorizes and looks up global protein folds, is likewise weak and largely contradicted by the data. While it is plausible that a model trained on hundreds of millions of natural proteins could memorize full sequence topologies, the authors' sequence masking experiments demonstrate that global context is not what drives contact predictions. When residues are unmasked randomly across the full sequence to provide a sparse, global signature of the fold, contact recovery is roughly twice as slow as when unmasking contiguous local regions around the contact site. In addition, storing full pairwise dependencies for every known protein family without modular compression would demand an estimated 261 billion parameters, an order of magnitude larger than the scale where ESM2's contact prediction performance actually plateaus. While the authors cannot rule out that long contiguous stretches occasionally help identify a protein, the model clearly does not require a full fold blueprint to establish correct residue contacts.

Hypothesis 3, that the model looks up segment pairings conditioned on sequence motifs and relative spacing, emerges as the strongest and most well supported explanation. The masking experiments reveal a clear threshold effect: contacts between residues or distant secondary structure elements recover sharply once a local flanking window of roughly 20 to 40 residues is provided on each side. Furthermore, removing intervening sequence entirely and adjusting rotary positional embeddings causes only a minor drop in performance, confirming that intermediate residues and global fold scaffolding are unnecessary for the model to register that two motifs interact. This motif pairing framework also aligns well with the model's actual parameter efficiency; estimating roughly four sparse contacts per position across known families requires around 4 billion parameters, which mirrors the 3 billion parameter threshold where ESM2 begins to saturate. Together, these results provide compelling evidence that ESM2 functions primarily as a compressed lookup engine for coevolving sequence motifs rather than a global fold catalog or a biophysical simulator.

## 2. Explain and evaluate the categorical Jacobian (Zhang et al.)

*In your own words, explain how Zhang et al.'s Jacobian method works: what they change in the input, what they measure in the output, and how they turn that into a contact map. From one sequence, it reaches contact precision 0.80; a linear model fit to the family's whole alignment reaches 0.67. Why does it do better?*

8 answers.

Starting with one sequence of length L, Zhang et al. replace each residue, one position at a time, with each of the 20 amino-acid types and run every resulting sequence through ESM-2 (Fig. 4A). They measure how each substitution changes the output logits—the scores before conversion into amino-acid probabilities—for every amino-acid type at every position, producing a four-dimensional tensor with dimensions L by 20 by L by 20. They mean-center and symmetrize this tensor, summarize each position pair’s 20-by-20 block using its Frobenius norm, and apply average-product correction to remove broad background effects, yielding a ranked contact map (Fig. 4A; Methods, Eqs. 5–7). Actual amino-acid substitutions matter: small numerical perturbations to the encoded input extract weaker contact information from this nonlinear model (Fig. 5A–D). The reported 0.80 versus 0.67 is average precision across 1,431 proteins for the top L/2 predicted contacts separated by more than 24 residues, with experimental contacts defined by a distance below 10 angstroms between their alpha-carbon atoms (Fig. 4C; Methods). The crucial explanation is that ESM-2 receives one sequence at inference, but its parameters already contain information learned from a large sequence corpus, whereas the inverse-covariance baseline estimates couplings from one family’s finite alignment. Fig. 4B shows a concrete advantage: residue types absent at particular alignment positions leave couplings unobservable for the alignment model, while ESM-2 supplies dependencies for all residue types; pooling information across learned motifs is therefore a plausible explanation for its better estimates. I judge the predictive improvement well established on this benchmark, but its exact cause remains incompletely isolated: the paper does not experimentally separate broader training data, motif reuse, and nonlinear modeling, and the Jacobian still trails the supervised Contact Head’s 0.87 precision (Fig. 4D).

---

The categorical Jacobian introduced by Zhang et al. is an unsupervised perturbation framework designed to extract residue to residue coevolutionary couplings directly from a protein language model without relying on any supervised contact prediction heads. Rather than inspecting internal attention maps or training an external classifier, the authors probe the model as a black box by observing how input perturbations ripple into output predictions. They begin with a wild type protein sequence of length $L$ and systematically mutate every single residue to each of the twenty standard amino acids. The authors highlight that using discrete, categorical token replacements is critical: making infinitesimal, continuous gradient perturbations to one hot vectors fails to overcome the nonlinear saturation inside deep transformer layers, whereas swapping the actual categorical token drives clear changes in the output representations.

For each of these mutated sequences, the authors perform a forward pass through ESM2 and capture the full matrix of unnormalized output logits across all sequence positions. Subtracting the mutant logits from the wild type logits measures how forcing a specific amino acid at one position shifts the model's likelihood for every amino acid at every other position. Collecting these differences across all mutations yields a four dimensional tensor of shape $L \times 20 \times L \times 20$. To collapse this tensor into an interpretable two dimensional contact map, they first mean center and symmetrize the pairwise couplings, then compute the Frobenius norm across each twenty by twenty amino acid block to obtain a single interaction strength for every residue pair. Finally, they apply standard Average Product Correction to remove background phylogenetic noise and entropy biases, leaving a clean matrix of predicted pairwise contacts.

In their benchmark of 1,431 proteins, this single sequence categorical Jacobian attains an average contact precision of 0.80, substantially outperforming a standard linear inverse covariance model fit to the entire multiple sequence alignment of the family, which only reaches 0.61. This dramatic improvement stems from how each method acquires and processes evolutionary information. A linear model, whether framed as a Markov Random Field or a multivariate Gaussian, is trained from scratch on a single alignment in isolation. If that alignment has few sequences, contains alignment errors, or suffers from phylogenetic sampling bias, the estimated inverse covariance matrix becomes noisy and requires heavy shrinkage regularization to avoid spurious correlations. In contrast, ESM2 has been pretrained on hundreds of millions of diverse natural protein sequences spanning thousands of evolutionary families. Through this massive pretraining, the language model has absorbed generalized biophysical priors such as secondary structure propensities, steric packings, and recurring motif architectures that transcend individual sequence alignments.

Furthermore, deep protein language models process context nonlinearly. In a linear model, disentangling direct physical couplings from transitive, indirect correlations—such as residue A correlating with residue C merely because both interact with residue B—relies entirely on a flat matrix inversion. ESM2 instead uses stacked multi head self attention layers that evaluate combinations of sequence positions jointly, enabling it to filter out indirect covariation far more effectively. Lastly, while a classical alignment model fits an average, static coupling matrix for an entire protein family, the categorical Jacobian evaluates coupling dynamics conditioned directly on the specific query sequence and its local flanking context. By evaluating mutations within the exact structural and sequence environment of that specific protein, the model produces sharper, more accurate contact predictions than a flat alignment wide average could ever provide.

To build the contact map, for each position pair in the sequence (L x L pairs), we compute the norm of the A x A block, which measures the correlation of each set of amino acid pairs appearing at those two positions. The authors note that in an MSA, some residues are not observed at every position, so the couplings cannot be computed, but a pLM infers a pairwise coupling for every residue at every position, which can be captured by the categorical Jacobian. The categorical Jacobian is an evaluation metric that measures how much entries of the probability vector changes as you impute a single amino acid of the sequence. Since the amino acids are categorical, this object is a 4 dimensional tensor: two dimensions i,j tell you which amino acid entry i you are changing and to which amino acid j, and the other two dimensions k,l tell you what is the change in probability for an entry k to change into amino acid l.

In order to score how well two sequence entries relate, we essentially average the Jacobian computation (value squared) over all 20^2 possible choices of possible amino acids that each entry could take. An average product correction is also applied. Pairwise coupling weights from MSA approaches (e.g. linear MRF/MG’s) are also averaged in a similar way. Contact precision computed from these contact maps for the categorical Jacobian approach is better. One possible reason that only couplings calculated from a set number of sequences in an MSA, some residue types may not be observed in every position, and so not all coupling weights are inferred (Figure 4C). Meanwhile, all pairwise contacts, including interactions which may never appear in the data, are inferred. There may be more of an overfitting problem with the linear methods: it’ll try to recover the interactions we see in one MSA but (maybe) not another, but the Jacobian computation benefits from a model that is implicitly learning pairwise interactions from patterns seen in proteins in different sequence alignments.

The categorical Jacobian is built as follows. For A possible amino acids and a sequence of length L, we mutate each residue in the sequence to each of the A amino acids for a total of L x A mutated sequences. For each of the mutated sequences, we evaluate the new logit (how likely the model thinks that amino acid in that position is) for each of the L residues and each of the A amino acids and compute the difference from the original logit. The differences are the entries of the categorical Jacobian tensor J, which is L x A x Lx A.

To get the contact map, for each pair of sequence indices (i,j) we get an A x A matrix J(i, :, j, :). Then we take the Frobenius norm of each of these to get an L x L contact map. The contact precision is lower for the linear model because it only uses information about pairwise couplings.

## 4. Why function tasks stop improving early (Li et al., with Zhang et al.)

*In Li et al., secondary structure is the only task that keeps improving with layer depth, model size, and pretraining. Most function tasks stop improving after the first 3-5 layers of models with 30+ layers. Why? You may draw on Zhang et al.'s account of what ESM-2 stores. Then propose an experiment that separates what the model knows from what the probe can read.*

6 answers.

Li et al. find that secondary structure is the only aligned task, and cite Zhang et al. in their discussion. Zhang et al. give the reason, since what the model stores is coevolution between motif pairs, which is mostly structural information. Function tasks depend less on this information, so they gain little beyond the first few layers. The main concern in Li et al. is the probe. The representation is mean-pooled over the whole sequence and fed to a linear model. For GB1 and AAV the signal is in a few mutated positions, and averaging over 265 or about 735 residues dilutes it. The early plateau could come partly from this pooling rather than from the features themselves. The authors list this as a limitation, but it directly affects the main conclusion for the tasks where scaling fails. Given this probe issue, a natural first step is to repeat the layer-by-layer study for GB1 and AAV with a probe that only uses the representations at the mutated positions instead of mean pooling. If the early plateau stays, the low-level feature conclusion becomes much stronger. If deeper layers start to help, part of the failure belongs to the probe. The two papers also suggest a direct test. GB1 is about epistasis between four sites, which is a pairwise coupling question. The categorical Jacobian of ESM-2 gives couplings between exactly those sites. A useful check would be whether these couplings predict the measured epistasis in the GB1 data. If they do not, that would be a concrete case where stored coevolution is not the information a fitness task needs, which is what Li et al. suggest.

---

One possible explanation is that early layers learn basic sequence features, such as amino acid composition and local sequence patterns, that are useful across many tasks, while later layers learn more specialized relationships. Zhang et al. provide a possible explanation for what happens in deeper layers. Their results suggest that ESM-2 learns evolutionary dependencies between interacting sequence motifs, which are useful for predicting structural contacts. This information is closely related to protein structure but may not directly predict biological function. Consequently, additional pretraining may improve the model's representation of structural relationships without producing equally useful information for function prediction.

I would train both linear probes and small nonlinear neural networks on representations from different layers, using the same training data. If both linear and nonlinear probes show little improvement in deeper layers, this would support the idea that later pretraining does not add much information relevant to the function task. ## 5. Compare the two papers' hypotheses (both papers)

Zhang et al. propose three hypotheses about how ESM-2 predicts protein structures:

Li et al. examine four possible explanations for why pretrained protein language models perform well on downstream tasks:
1.Pretraining teaches the model meaningful biological features that can be reused for other prediction tasks.
2.The architecture and large number of parameters explain some of the performance improvement, even without pretraining.
3.Pretraining helps mainly by producing favorable weight distributions or initialization scales.
4.Downstream tasks benefit mainly from simple features learned early during pretraining, rather than increasingly complex features learned later.

I would compare Zhang et al.'s Hypothesis 2 (full-fold lookup) with Li et al.'s Hypothesis 1 (feature reuse). The main difference is that Zhang's hypothesis emphasizes recognizing familiar structural patterns, while Li's hypothesis emphasizes learning generalizable representations that can be useful for different prediction tasks. To test these ideas, I would evaluate a pretrained protein language model on proteins with structures that are very different from those represented in its training data. The full-fold lookup hypothesis predicts that performance would decrease when the model encounters unfamiliar folds, because it cannot easily match them to previously learned structures. In contrast, the general feature reuse hypothesis suggests that the model could still perform well if it has learned transferable biological features that apply to unfamiliar proteins.

Zhang et al. proposes the following three hypotheses: 1) ESM-2 has learned the physics of protein folding, 2) ESM-2 has learned a coevolution model for each protein family, and 3) ESM-2 stores small coevolution models for pairs of fragments (which can be shared across families). The first hypothesis is disproven by a consistent error on isoform predictions (they point to myoglobin as an example, where ESM-2 is inconsistent with predicting structure in water). The results in Zhang et al point to their third hypothesis being the most likely. In comparison, Li et al. test four hypotheses for how pretrained PLMs improve performance on downstream tasks: 1) feature reuse across tasks, 2) inductive biases from the pretrained model, 3) initialization of weights to the proper scale, and 4) only low-level features learned early in pretraining are the most critical to downstream performance. Both the first hypothesis of Zhang et al. with the first hypothesis of Li et al. predict that PLMs are able to generalize to a broad set of tasks.
