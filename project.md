## What to do

Your project can be any of these:

- Apply an existing AI/ML method to a scientific problem, especially one from your own dissertation. Compare against a baseline and show that it works, or show where and why it doesn't.
- Develop a new method for an open scientific challenge. Show that it does something existing methods don't, on a problem where you can measure it.
- Reproduce a recent AI-in-science paper with your own implementation, then go past it: a new dataset, a failure case, an ablation, or a stronger baseline. Just confirming the paper isn't enough.
- Write a literature review of an emerging area at the AI/ML---science intersection. Compare the papers against each other instead of summarizing them one at a time.

I especially like projects that could turn into a research paper. If I waived your homework (see the [syllabus](syllabus.html#homework)), the project replaces it and I'll expect more.

Work alone or in a team of two. A team should do more, and say who did what. The work should be computational. You don't have to collect new experimental data, but you can.

## <a name="proposal"></a>Proposal --- Fri, Nov 6

Half a page to one page, as a PDF on Gradescope. One per team, with both names. Include:

1. The problem, and why it matters.
2. The data or simulator, and whether you already have access to it. If you don't, say how you'll get it. This is where projects most often stall.
3. What you'll have to show at the end (a result, a comparison, a reproduced figure, a review), what would count as success, and what you'll do if it doesn't work.
4. Who you're working with, if anyone.

I'll read every proposal and reply, usually about scope. You can change direction afterward. If you're not sure whether an idea is too big, send it anyway.

## <a name="presentation"></a>Presentation --- Mon, Nov 30 -- Fri, Dec 4

Each project gets at least 20 minutes, including questions. I'll set the exact length and the order once the proposals are in. Plan on about three-quarters talk and one-quarter questions.

Everyone presents. If you're on a team, each of you speaks for part of the slot.

In the talk, say what the question is, why it's hard, what you did, what you found, and what you'd do next. Show at least one figure that carries the result. Say what didn't work. "I didn't test that" is a fine answer to a question.

Send me your slides as a PDF by 9pm the night before your slot.

## <a name="comments"></a>Comments on classmates' presentations

When you're in the audience, fill out a Google Form for each talk. It asks for two things: something you liked, and a suggestion to make it better. Be specific. What would you try, or what would you want to see? I'll post the link before the presentations start.

The comments aren't graded. They're there to help the authors before the report is due. There are no comments on the written reports.

## <a name="report"></a>Report --- Fri, Dec 18, 2:00pm

The registrar sets the due date for final projects, and ours is Fri, Dec 18 at 2:00pm, during the final exam period. Write it like a workshop paper: 6--8 pages plus references, as a PDF on Gradescope. One per team.

- Put the question and your claim in the first paragraph.
- Describe what you did well enough that someone could repeat it: data, model, hyperparameters, baselines, and how you evaluated. Link your code.
- Show the results in at least one table or figure, with a baseline.
- Say what doesn't work and what you'd check next. Partial and negative results are fine if you report them honestly.
- For a literature review, replace the methods and results with a comparison of the papers: what each one claims, what evidence supports it, and where they disagree.
- On a team, add a short paragraph on who did what.
- Note at the top which AI tools you used and where. Use whatever tools you like, but be able to explain what your code and your tools are doing.

## How I'll evaluate it

- Is the question clear, and does the evidence support the claim?
- Is there a sensible baseline, and are the results reported honestly, including what failed?
- Could someone repeat it from the report?
- Do you understand the method and the science, or just that the code ran?

A modest result on a hard question is fine.
