## What to do

Pick one of four shapes. Each one needs a question you can say in a sentence and an end state you can show.

- **Apply an existing method to a scientific problem**, especially one from your own dissertation. Show the method working, or show clearly where and why it fails, against a sensible baseline.
- **Develop a new method** for an open scientific challenge. Show that it does something an existing method does not, on a problem where you can measure it.
- **Reproduce a recent AI-in-science paper** with a new implementation, and push past it: a new dataset, a failure case, an ablation, or a stronger baseline. A reproduction that only confirms the paper is not enough.
- **Write a literature review** of an emerging area at the AI/ML--science intersection. It should compare papers against each other, not summarize them one at a time.

Projects with a path to a research article are especially encouraged. If I waived your homework (see the [syllabus](syllabus.html#homework)), the project replaces it and I expect it to be more substantial.

You may work alone or in a team of two. A team is expected to do correspondingly more and to say who did what. Computational work is expected; you need not collect new experimental data, but you may.

## <a name="proposal"></a>Proposal --- Fri, Nov 6

Between half a page and one page, as a PDF on Gradescope. One proposal per team; put both names on it. Say four things:

1. **The problem**, and why it matters.
2. **The data or simulator** you will use, and whether you already have access to it. If you do not, say how you will get it. Projects stall most often here.
3. **What you will have to show at the end**: a result, a comparison, a reproduced figure, a review. Say what would count as success, and what you will do if it does not work.
4. **Who you are working with**, if anyone.

I read every proposal and reply with feedback, usually on scope. You can change direction afterward; the point is to commit to something concrete early. If you are unsure whether an idea is too big, send it anyway.

## <a name="presentation"></a>Presentation --- Mon, Nov 30 -- Fri, Dec 4

Each project gets a slot of **at least 20 minutes, including questions**. The exact length is set once I know how many projects there are, and I will announce it with the order. Plan about three-quarters of the slot for the talk and the rest for questions.

**Everyone presents, including teams.** A team gets one slot, and each member speaks for part of it.

A good talk answers these in order: what is the question, why is it hard, what did you do, what did you find, and what would you do next. Show at least one figure that carries the result. Say what did not work. You will be asked questions; "I did not test that" is a fine answer.

Send your slides as a PDF to me by 9pm the night before your slot.

## <a name="report"></a>Report --- due in the final exam period

The registrar sets the due date for final projects, and it falls in the final exam period (Dec 11--19). I will post the date here as soon as it is assigned. Written like a workshop paper: **6--8 pages plus references**, as a PDF on Gradescope. One report per team.

- **State the question and the claim** in the first paragraph. A reader should know what you found before page two.
- **Say what you did precisely enough to repeat it**: data, model, hyperparameters, baselines, and how you evaluated. Link your code (a repository or a notebook).
- **Show the result** with at least one table or figure, and a baseline to compare it to.
- **Say what does not work** and what you would check next. Negative and partial results are fine if they are honestly reported.
- **For a literature review**, replace the method and results with a comparison of the papers: what each claims, what evidence supports it, and where they disagree.
- **For a team**, add a short paragraph on who did what.
- **Note at the top which AI tools you used and where.** You may use whatever tools you like, and you must be able to explain what your code and your tools are doing.

## <a name="comments"></a>Comments on classmates' reports

After the reports are in, you read two or three classmates' reports and write constructive comments, so authors can refine their work. The aim is useful suggestions: what was convincing, what was not, and what you would try. Reading and commenting is part of the project grade. Assignments and the deadline are announced in class.

## How the project is evaluated

For the written report and the talk:

- Is the question clear, and is the claim supported by the evidence shown?
- Is there a sensible baseline, and are the results honestly reported, including what failed?
- Could someone repeat the work from the report?
- Does it show that you understand the method and the science, not just that it ran?

Ambition counts. A modest result on a hard question is fine; a polished result on a trivial one is not.
